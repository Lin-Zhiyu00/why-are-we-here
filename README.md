From my perspective, code is ubiquitous – found across a vast array of everyday domains and in seemingly invisible details. Generally speaking, everything from communication networks and real-world tasks—such as the common practice of people using computers for communication or installing software—to the most minute aspects, like the websites we visit, each click, or every link redirect, is closely intertwined with code.
At the same time, in today's rapidly evolving society, as an art student, I also wonder whether code can, to some extent, embody "art." I believe the answer is unequivocally yes. While people often draw a clear line between code – seen as part of the STEM fields – and the word "art," it is evident that most artistic works today utilize code to create fascinating visual effects and aesthetic experiences.As illustrated by Conway's Game of Life, the code itself possesses its own "life" —it spreads in an irregular, complex, and unpredictable manner. This perhaps offers an answer to the question, "Why are we here?" When an artist masters a certain level of coding proficiency, they can give life to their own ideas and use them to unleash a wild creative process, giving rise to even more designs.
The uncertainties and possibilities inherent in code-based creation align perfectly with the creative approaches artists typically employ when working with other materials; the results—unpredictable, uniquely crafted, and sometimes even surpassing all expectations—can, to some extent, challenge our conventional understanding.This artistic quality also supports Dylan Beattie's perspective, as he argues in *The Art of Code* that code is not merely a cold tool, but rather an artistic medium possessing both growth potential and expressive power; simple underlying rules can give rise to complex, unpredictable visual and formal beauty – this is precisely the core appeal of code art (Beattie, 2020).Furthermore, according to Don Ihde's media theory, technological media are never neutral; different creative media reshape the creator's perception, imagination, and boundaries of creation. Failure to engage with code as a medium means missing its unique generative and contingent creative value and limiting the dimensions of one's artistic expression (Ihde, 1990).
Given the rapid advancement of AI technology today, AI makes it much easier for us to learn about and apply AI – as demonstrated by my own graduation project involving a fish-shaped light installation: I needed to make the device swing at varying amplitudes. Prior to this, I had no prior experience with writing code for Arduino. During the short and hectic graduation season, attempting to learn through conventional methods and write the code myself would have been exceedingly challenging. However, AI not only helped me write the code directly but also provided valuable suggestions; whenever I wanted to make a change, it offered immediate feedback, and when an error occurred, it raised questions for me to investigate and resolve. Nevertheless, there remains one notable issue: AI has not yet evolved to be entirely error-free; it still encounters certain difficulties when dealing with technical terminology or operational aspects. This, in turn, serves as another answer to the question "Why are we here?": AI handles the production side, while I always need to understand the underlying principles, discern the quality of the output, or identify any bugs.In the future, AI may evolve to a point of being virtually invincible; in such a scenario, artists' most pure ideas would become the most valuable, yet we could still realize our creative visions in simpler or more straightforward ways.
Ultimately, I have always believed that learning to code could even help me break free from the rigid patterns of creative thinking;the sheer number of unexpected, unexplored fields and the vast creative platform—that's why I'm here, and why I am so eager to learn, and why I harbor such deep curiosity and a strong desire to explore this realm.
